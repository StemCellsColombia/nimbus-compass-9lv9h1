What the cells do

What the cells may do after a brain injury

Mesenchymal stem cells work mostly by the signals they release, and that is what the research is testing after brain injury.

01

Calm the inflammation that keeps going

After a head injury, the brain's immune cells (microglia) can stay switched on long after the accident, and that inflammation keeps harming nearby tissue. The cells release signals that may calm it. For the patient, the aim is to stop the injury from quietly spreading.

02

Support the neurons still working

The cells release growth factors (natural proteins that help nerve cells survive). They may help protect the neurons around the injury that are struggling but still alive.

03

Help the brain find new paths

The brain can build new connections to take over lost tasks; this is called neuroplasticity. The cells are studied for how they may support that process, while rehabilitation trains the new paths.

04

Timing and route matter

In animal studies, the cells worked best when given early after the injury. That is why, for recent injuries in patients under 65, the neurologist may consider the intrathecal route, into the fluid that bathes the brain and spinal cord.

What the evidence says

What the research shows so far

Human research with mesenchymal stem cells in traumatic brain injury is growing: several trials, including a larger one with a modified cell, build on a strong base of animal studies.

Lab & animal studiesFirst human studiesSeveral clinical trialsStandard of care

In a small first study, Wharton's jelly mesenchymal stem cells were well tolerated, and spasticity, strength and independence scores improved over one year.

Phase I study, 6 young adults with brain injury, cells given into the spinal fluid, by vein and into the muscle. Only brief, mild side effects were reported.

Safety and efficiency of Wharton's Jelly-derived mesenchymal stem cell administration in patients with traumatic brain injury: First results of a phase I study. · World Journal of Stem Cells, 2024 ↗

People with lasting effects of a brain injury who received umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells into the spinal fluid improved in movement and self-care scores; the untreated group did not.

Randomized study, 40 patients with sequelae of brain injury, four applications by lumbar puncture, 6 months of follow-up, compared with a group that did not receive cells.

Umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell transplantation significantly improves neurological function in patients with sequelae of traumatic brain injury. · Brain Research, 2013 ↗

In chronic brain injury, a modified mesenchymal cell implanted into the brain by surgery improved movement scores more than a sham operation at 6 months.

Randomized, sham-controlled phase 2 trial, 63 patients with stable motor problems after brain injury. This used a modified bone-marrow cell product and brain surgery, a different treatment from the Stem Cells Colombia protocol.

Cell Therapy for Chronic TBI: Interim Analysis of the Randomized Controlled STEMTRA Trial. · Neurology, 2021 ↗

Across 80 animal studies, mesenchymal cells improved movement and memory after brain injury, with the best results when given in the first week.

Systematic review and meta-analysis of preclinical studies in rodents.

Systematic review and meta-analysis of preclinical studies testing mesenchymal stromal cells for traumatic brain injury. · NPJ Regenerative Medicine, 2021 ↗

In short: Studies with umbilical cord and Wharton’s jelly cells showed improvements in movement, spasticity and independence, and a large body of animal research supports these results. Questions

Your questions, answered.

Am I (or is my family member) a candidate?

It depends on how long ago the injury happened, the imaging, the current symptoms and general health. Send the reports; a neurologist reviews them for free, so the plan is designed for the patient before you book anything.

What do the stem cells actually do after a brain injury?

They release signals that may calm the inflammation that keeps going after the injury, support the neurons that are still working and help the brain build new connections. The aim is to support recovery, together with rehabilitation.

Does it matter how long ago the injury was?

Yes. For patients under 65 with an injury diagnosed within the last year, the neurologist may consider the intrathecal route. After that, the intravenous route can be considered, and for long-standing injuries too the neurologist reviews the case and designs the plan.

When would you see changes?

Changes usually build between 3 and 6 months, and every person responds at their own pace. Keep up the rehabilitation: it turns that support into real progress.

How many visits will be needed?

Two visits are usually recommended, 6 to 8 weeks apart. The neurologist confirms it for each case.

What does the neurologist need?

The imaging and medical reports, the date of the injury, current medication and recent blood work: CBC, vitamin B12, folate, HIV, hepatitis B and C and syphilis serology, TSH and free T4, fasting glucose and HbA1c, ferritin, creatinine, liver enzymes and vitamin D. Every protocol also includes cancer markers.

Do you stop current medication or therapy?

No. Anti-seizure medication, any other prescriptions and rehabilitation stay exactly as the treating doctors indicated. This works alongside them, never instead.

How do you keep the patient safe?

Every protocol starts with cancer markers, for your safety: it is designed for people without active cancer. The neurologist reviews the full history first.

Is it legal and safe?

Stem cell therapy is not approved by the FDA, but in Colombia it is legal. Every application is done by specialist physicians, after a neurologist has reviewed your case.

What does the intrathecal protocol look like?

Five days in Medellín. Day 1: cancer markers. Day 2: a blood detox IV and nebulized exosomes. Day 3: the intrathecal application of stem cells in the operating room, with nebulized exosomes. Days 4 and 5: exosomes IV and nebulized. The neurologist reviews your case first and decides whether this route is for you.

How much does it cost?

The intravenous protocol is from $5,850 USD all-inclusive, and the intrathecal protocol, for recent injuries, from $7,500. The exact price is confirmed after the neurologist consultation.
