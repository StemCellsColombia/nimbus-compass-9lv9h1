How does stem cell therapy actually work?

Not by turning into new body parts. Mesenchymal stem cells work mostly by sending signals. Here is what happens after the injection, step by step.

5 min readReviewed with the medical team of Stem Cells Colombia

The 30-second version
  • The cells used in clinics mostly work by sending signals, not by turning into new tissue.
  • Those signals calm inflammation, rebalance the immune system and help your own cells repair.
  • How the cells are given, into a joint or through a vein, depends on the problem you have.

Most people picture stem cell therapy like a hardware store. The cells arrive, find the broken part and become a shiny replacement. It is a lovely image. It is also mostly wrong.

The real story is more interesting, and it explains why serious clinics talk about what the cells “may” do, and why every plan is designed for the person. Here is what actually happens, in plain words.

Which stem cells are being discussed?

There are several families. Embryonic and reprogrammed cells can become almost anything, which makes them powerful in the lab and tricky in patients. Blood-forming cells from marrow are used in hospital transplants for certain blood diseases.

The cells used in regenerative clinics are mesenchymal stem cells. They are found in tissues like fat, bone marrow and the umbilical cord. Stem Cells Colombia uses only those from Wharton’s jelly, the soft tissue inside the cord.

In plain wordsMesenchymal

from the body’s connective tissue, the support structure behind bone, cartilage, fat and tendon

Do the cells turn into new tissue?

In a lab dish, mesenchymal cells can be coaxed into bone, cartilage or fat. In the body, most of them don’t stay long, and very few become new tissue.

That surprised scientists. One of the researchers who first named these cells now suggests calling them “medicinal signaling cells,” because the signals seem to be where most of the benefit comes from.

MythStem cells become brand-new cartilage, nerve or muscle.

Reality: Their main effect is the signals they release. Your own tissue does the rebuilding.

So what do the signals do?

Picture a neighborhood after a storm. The cells are not the builders. They are the dispatcher on the radio, telling the crews where to go and asking the sirens to quiet down.

  • Calming inflammation: they release molecules that turn down the chemicals behind pain and swelling.
  • Rebalancing the immune system: they nudge overactive immune cells toward a calmer state, which is why they are studied in autoimmune conditions.
  • Supporting repair: growth factors help local cells survive, form new small blood vessels and rebuild.
In plain wordsGrowth factors

proteins that tell nearby cells to grow, divide or heal

How do the cells get where they are needed?

It depends on the problem. For a knee, a shoulder or a disc, the cells go straight into the target, ideally under ultrasound or fluoroscopy so the doctor sees where they land.

For whole-body conditions, like some autoimmune or neurological problems, an intravenous infusion lets the signals reach the immune system as a whole. For certain neurological cases, cells can be placed into the fluid around the spinal cord.

Why is every plan personal?

Because the cells work with your body, not instead of it. How much inflammation you have, how damaged the tissue is, your age and your health all shape the response.

The way the cells are prepared and delivered matters too. That is why your specialist reviews your case first, so your plan is designed for you.

How long before anything changes?

Signals take time to change a tissue. Most studies measure results over weeks to months, not days, and improvement is usually expected at four to six months, building gradually.

That is also why follow-up matters. Your doctor compares where you started with where you are, so you can judge the result on facts instead of on a good or bad afternoon.

Tip

Before treatment, write down three things that are hard for you today, like stairs, sleep or a walk. Check them again at four to six months.

What this means for you
  1. Expect the cells to calm inflammation and support your body’s own repair.
  2. Ask how the cells will reach your problem, and how the doctor will see the target.
  3. Give it time; signal-driven changes are gradual, over weeks to months.
Where the evidence stands
Lab & animal studiesFirst human studiesSeveral clinical trialsStandard of care

The signaling role of mesenchymal cells is well described in laboratory work and widely accepted by researchers. Clinical trials in several conditions have shown benefits, and research is growing. Pooled trials of intravenous mesenchymal cells show a favorable safety profile.

Questions

Do stem cells turn into new cartilage or nerves?

Their main effect comes from the signals they release, which calm inflammation and help your own cells repair.

How long do the cells stay in the body?

Studies suggest most don’t stay long. The signals they release seem to start changes that continue after the cells are gone.

Is an IV better than an injection into the joint?

They do different jobs. A targeted injection aims at one area; an IV aims at the immune system as a whole. Your specialist chooses based on your condition.

Is stem cell therapy approved by the FDA?

Stem cell therapy is not approved by the FDA, but in Colombia it is legal.
