What the cells do

What the cells may do in an arthritic joint

In both kinds of arthritis, inflammation keeps the pain going and wears the joint down. Mesenchymal stem cells work mostly through the signals they release, which is why they are studied for both.

01

Calming the joint lining

In osteoarthritis, the thin lining inside the joint (the synovium) becomes inflamed and keeps the joint swollen and sore. Placed inside the joint, the cells release signals that may help quiet that inflammation, which could mean less pain and stiffness.

02

A better environment for cartilage

Cartilage is the smooth tissue that lets bones glide. The growth factors the cells release are studied for how they may protect the cartilage that remains from the inflammation around it. In studies, the benefit shows most where you feel it: pain and daily function.

03

Rebalancing the immune response

In rheumatoid arthritis, the immune system attacks the joints from the inside. Given intravenously, the cells are studied for how they may support the regulatory immune cells that act as a brake, aiming to calm the attack without switching the immune system off.

04

Placed where the problem is

That is why the path changes. In osteoarthritis the problem is inside the joint, so the cells go there, under ultrasound. In rheumatoid arthritis the problem is in the whole body, so the cells travel with the blood.

What the evidence says

What the research shows so far

Every study shown here was checked on PubMed, with the kind of cells each one used.

Lab & animal studiesFirst human studiesSeveral clinical trialsStandard of care

Stem cell injections inside the knee eased pain and improved daily function compared with control treatments.

Systematic review and meta-analysis of 28 randomized trials in knee osteoarthritis, in studies using mesenchymal stem cells from several sources. Reactions were short-lived and limited to the injected joint.

Efficacy and safety of intra-articular mesenchymal stem cell-based therapies in knee osteoarthritis: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. · Clinical Rheumatology, 2026 ↗

Umbilical cord stem cells in the knee were safe, and function scores improved at 6 months.

Systematic review and meta-analysis of 8 clinical studies with 688 people with knee osteoarthritis treated with umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells. Lower doses showed the clearest pain relief, and side effects were mild and short-lived.

Regenerative Therapy in Osteoarthritis Using Umbilical Cord-Origin Mesenchymal Stem Cells: A Critical Appraisal of Clinical Safety and Efficacy Through Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis. · Stem Cells International, 2025 ↗

Across 36 randomized trials in arthritis, stem cells eased joint pain without raising the rate of side effects.

Systematic review and meta-analysis covering osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis and other inflammatory arthritis, in studies using mesenchymal stem cells. For rheumatoid arthritis the authors describe a potential benefit.

Efficacy and safety of culture-expanded mesenchymal stromal cell therapy in the treatment of 4 types of inflammatory arthritis: A systematic review and meta-analysis of 36 randomized controlled trials. · Seminars in Arthritis and Rheumatism, 2024 ↗

In rheumatoid arthritis, disease activity improved in the 3 to 6 months after a single intravenous infusion, with acceptable safety over five years.

Five-year follow-up of 9 patients with rheumatoid arthritis who received a single infusion of umbilical cord blood mesenchymal stem cells. The authors suggest repeated doses may help extend the effect.

Long-term safety of intravenous human umbilical cord blood-derived mesenchymal stem cell therapy in rheumatoid arthritis: a 5-year follow-up study. · Stem Cells Translational Medicine, 2026 ↗

In short: For osteoarthritis, many randomized trials show help with pain and daily function. For rheumatoid arthritis, human studies are encouraging and growing. Your specialist or rheumatologist reviews your studies for free and explains what you can expect.

Questions

Your questions, answered.

Am I a candidate?

Send your X-ray or MRI, and your blood work if you have rheumatoid arthritis. A specialist reviews them for free and tells you which path fits you. Grades 1 to 3 osteoarthritis respond best, and grade 4 (“bone on bone”) can also be treated, after your specialist reviews your images.

How do I know which arthritis I have?

Your diagnosis and your studies tell. Osteoarthritis shows on an X-ray or MRI; rheumatoid arthritis shows in blood tests such as rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP. Send what you have and a specialist tells you which path fits.

What do the cells actually do?

In osteoarthritis, placed inside the joint, they release signals that may calm inflammation and support the cartilage that remains. In rheumatoid arthritis, given intravenously, they are studied for how they may help rebalance the immune response. The aim is less pain and better use of your joints.

When could I notice a change?

For a joint, improvement usually builds gradually, between 4 and 6 months after the application. For rheumatoid arthritis, results are usually seen between 3 and 6 months after the intravenous protocol.

How many visits will I need?

Osteoarthritis in one joint is treated in a single day in Medellín, under local anesthesia, and several joints can be treated on the same day. For rheumatoid arthritis, the most complete protocol combines a local application in the affected joints with the four-day IV; some patients do only one of the two, depending on the doctors’ review. For rheumatoid arthritis, two visits are usually recommended, the second 6 to 8 weeks after the first.

What studies do you need?

For osteoarthritis, an X-ray or MRI of the joint. For rheumatoid arthritis, X-rays of the affected joints, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP, ESR, CRP, anti-RA33 and ANA. Every intravenous protocol also includes cancer markers.

Do I stop my arthritis medication?

Never on your own. Your rheumatologist or specialist reviews your current medication in your consultation and tells you exactly what to do. After a joint application, you take only what your doctor recommends, and no anti-inflammatories (NSAIDs).

How do you keep me safe?

Every intravenous protocol starts with cancer markers, for your safety: the treatment is designed for people without active cancer. Your specialist also reviews your general health and the joint itself, so your plan is designed for you.

Is it legal and safe?

Stem cell therapy is not approved by the FDA, but in Colombia it is legal. Joint applications are done by specialist physicians in a fully equipped operating room, under ultrasound guidance. Afterwards you rest at the hotel, one elevator away, with your coordinator checking on you.

How much does it cost?

Osteoarthritis treatment starts from $3,790 USD for a small joint, $3,990 for a knee or shoulder and $4,150 for a hip; two large joints start from $6,500. The rheumatoid arthritis protocol starts from $5,850 USD. All-inclusive, with your exact price confirmed after your consultation.
