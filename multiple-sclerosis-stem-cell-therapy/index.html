What the cells do

What the cells may do in multiple sclerosis

Mesenchymal stem cells work mostly by the signals they release, which is why they are studied for the immune side of MS.

01

Calm the immune attack

In MS, some immune cells mistake myelin (the coating that insulates your nerves) for an enemy. Mesenchymal stem cells release signals that may push the immune system toward regulatory T cells, the cells that tell an attack to stand down. For you, the aim is fewer flare-ups and less new damage.

02

Lower inflammation in the brain and spinal cord

Ongoing inflammation is what keeps adding new lesions (the spots seen on your MRI). The cells are studied for how they may lower that inflammation. Less inflammation could mean more stable scans and a steadier course.

03

Support the nerve cells still working

The cells release growth factors (natural proteins that help nerve cells survive). They may help protect the nerves and the myelin-making cells that are still alive. The goal is to keep more of the function you have today.

04

Closer to the target, for some

Given intrathecally, the cells go into the cerebrospinal fluid, the fluid that bathes your brain and spinal cord. In one trial with mesenchymal stem cells, this route showed extra benefits over the vein. The neurologist decides whether it is right for you.

What the evidence says

What the research shows so far

Mesenchymal stem cells for MS have been tested in small and medium-sized human trials, by vein and into the spinal fluid. The results are encouraging, and the research keeps growing.

Lab & animal studiesFirst human studiesSeveral clinical trialsStandard of care

Umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells given by vein were well tolerated, and several measures of disability and quality of life improved.

Safety and feasibility study, 20 people with MS, one year of follow-up. No serious side effects were reported.

Clinical feasibility of umbilical cord tissue-derived mesenchymal stem cells in the treatment of multiple sclerosis. · Journal of Translational Medicine, 2018 ↗

Umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells given into the spinal fluid were well tolerated, and the general disability score improved at 6 months.

Phase I/II dose-finding study in people with MS, comparing one and two intrathecal doses. Two doses showed more improvements, including in lesion load and hand dexterity.

Human Umbilical Cord-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells in the Treatment of Multiple Sclerosis Patients: Phase I/II Dose-Finding Clinical Study. · Cell Transplantation, 2024 ↗

Fewer patients got worse with mesenchymal stem cells than with a sham treatment, and the intrathecal route did better than the vein in several measures.

Randomized, sham-controlled trial in 48 people with active progressive MS, using the patients' own bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, by vein or into the spinal fluid, over 14 months.

Beneficial effects of autologous mesenchymal stem cell transplantation in active progressive multiple sclerosis. · Brain, 2020 ↗

Across 30 studies, cells given into the spinal fluid showed a small improvement in disability compared with placebo, and the treatment was generally safe.

Systematic review and meta-analysis of studies using mesenchymal stem cells in relapsing and progressive MS.

Neurological efficacy and safety of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) therapy in people with multiple sclerosis (pwMS): An updated systematic review and meta-analysis. · Multiple Sclerosis and Related Disorders, 2024 ↗

In short: The safety record in these studies is good, and several trials showed improvements in disability and quality of life, most clearly when the cells were given into the spinal fluid. A neurologist reviews your case for free, and the cells work alongside your disease-modifying therapy.

Questions

Your questions, answered.

Am I a candidate?

It depends on your type of MS, how long ago you were diagnosed, your MRI and your general health. Send your reports; a neurologist reviews them for free, so your plan is designed for you before you book anything.

What do the stem cells actually do in MS?

They release signals that may calm the immune attack on your myelin (the coating of your nerves) and lower inflammation in the brain and spinal cord. They are also studied for helping nerve cells survive. The aim is to help slow the disease and protect the function you have.

IV or intrathecal: which one will I get?

The neurologist decides. The intravenous route suits most patients, at any stage. The intrathecal route, into the fluid around the spinal cord, is for patients under 65 diagnosed within the last year, and it is done in the operating room.

When would I see results?

Changes usually build between 3 and 6 months, and every body responds at its own pace. Your neurologist follows your progress with you.

How many visits will I need?

Two visits are usually recommended: one now and a second 6 to 8 weeks later, repeating the protocol; returning patients receive a returning-patient discount on the second visit. The neurologist confirms it for your case.

What tests do you need?

Your latest MRI, your diagnosis date and recent blood work: CBC, vitamin B12, folate, HIV, hepatitis B and C and syphilis serology, TSH and free T4, fasting glucose and HbA1c, ferritin, creatinine, liver enzymes and vitamin D. Every protocol also includes cancer markers.

Do I stop my MS medication?

No. This works alongside the neurologist who treats you at home, never instead. Never stop or change a disease-modifying therapy on your own; your own neurologist leads that.

How do you keep me safe?

Every protocol starts with cancer markers, for your safety: it is designed for people without active cancer. The neurologist also reviews your full history first, so your plan is designed for you.

Is it legal and safe?

Stem cell therapy is not approved by the FDA, but in Colombia it is legal. Every application is done by specialist physicians, after a neurologist has reviewed your case.

What does the intrathecal protocol look like?

Five days in Medellín. Day 1: cancer markers. Day 2: a blood detox IV and nebulized exosomes. Day 3: the intrathecal application of stem cells in the operating room, with nebulized exosomes. Days 4 and 5: exosomes IV and nebulized. The neurologist reviews your case first and decides whether this route is for you.

How much does it cost?

The intravenous MS protocol is from $5,850 USD all-inclusive, and the intrathecal protocol from $7,500. Your exact price is confirmed after the neurologist consultation.
