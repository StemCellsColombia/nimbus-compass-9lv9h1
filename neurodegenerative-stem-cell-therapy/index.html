What the cells do

What the cells may do in a nervous system under strain

In neurodegenerative conditions, nerve cells are lost slowly, and long-lasting inflammation is part of that process. Mesenchymal stem cells work mostly through the signals they release.

01

Calming inflammation

The cells release signals that may quiet the immune cells of the brain and spinal cord when they stay switched on for too long. Less of that background inflammation is one way researchers hope to slow how fast damage builds up.

02

Growth-factor support

The cells produce neurotrophic factors: natural proteins that help nerve cells survive and keep working. The aim is to protect the nerve cells you have.

03

Protecting the cells still at work

Mesenchymal stem cells are studied for signals that may help nerve cells that are still working survive under stress, alongside their effect on inflammation.

04

Two ways to reach the nervous system

Given intravenously, the cells and their signals travel through the whole body. Given intrathecally, into the fluid around the spinal cord, they are placed closer to the nervous system; this route is only for some recent diagnoses.

What the evidence says

What the research shows so far

Every study shown here was checked on PubMed. Here is what they found.

Lab & animal studiesFirst human studiesSeveral clinical trialsStandard of care

Across many trials of cell therapies in neurodegenerative conditions, serious side effects were uncommon.

Systematic review with meta-analysis of 71 clinical studies of different cell types, including mesenchymal stem cells, in conditions such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disease. The authors call the results promising.

Are Cell-Based Therapies Safe and Effective in the Treatment of Neurodegenerative Diseases? A Systematic Review with Meta-Analysis. · Biomolecules, 2022 ↗

Wharton's jelly stem cells, given intravenously or intrathecally, were safe in a small group of people with ALS.

Phase I study of 12 patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), half by each route, two doses three months apart. Some patients reported improvements in swallowing, speech and muscle strength.

Intravenous vs intrathecal transplantation of allogeneic GMP/GCP compliant Wharton's jelly mesenchymal stromal cells in ALS patients: a phase I study. · Neurodegenerative Disease Management, 2025 ↗

In ALS, people who received Wharton's jelly stem cells into the spinal fluid lived longer than closely matched patients from earlier ALS trials, and some showed slower progression.

Case-control study in Poland, 67 people with ALS who received three intrathecal injections of Wharton’s jelly mesenchymal stem cells two months apart, compared with 67 matched patients from an international ALS trial database. No serious adverse reactions were observed.

Umbilical Cord Mesenchymal Stem Cells in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis: an Original Study. · Stem Cell Reviews and Reports, 2020 ↗

In short: Human studies consistently show the cells are well tolerated, and studies with Wharton’s jelly cells in ALS show encouraging signals, including longer survival than matched patients. A neurologist reviews every case and explains what you can expect.

Questions

Your questions, answered.

Am I a candidate?

A neurologist reviews your diagnosis, your reports and your recent blood work. Send what you have on WhatsApp, and you get a clear answer and a plan designed for you.

What do the cells actually do in a neurodegenerative condition?

They release signals that may help calm inflammation in the nervous system and support the nerve cells that are still working. The aim is to protect the nerve cells you have and help slow the progression.

IV or intrathecal: which one is for me?

Most patients receive the intravenous protocol, over four days. The intrathecal route, into the fluid around the spinal cord, is for patients under 65 diagnosed within the last year, and takes five days. The neurologist confirms which one fits you.

When could I notice a change?

Results are usually seen between 3 and 6 months after the protocol. Change is gradual, and the goal is a slower pace of the condition, measured by your own neurologist.

How many visits will I need?

Two visits are usually recommended, the second 6 to 8 weeks after the first. The neurologist tells you whether a second one makes sense for you and when.

What tests do you need?

CBC, vitamin B12, folate, HIV, hepatitis B and C and syphilis serology, TSH and free T4, fasting glucose and HbA1c, ferritin, creatinine, liver enzymes and vitamin D, plus your neurology reports and imaging. Every protocol also includes cancer markers on day one.

Do I stop my current medication?

No, never on your own. This is designed to go alongside the care you already receive. The neurologist reviews your medication in your consultation and tells you exactly what to do.

How do you keep me safe?

Every protocol starts with cancer markers, for your safety: it is designed for people without active cancer. The neurologist also reviews your general health, so your plan is designed for you.

Is it legal and safe?

Stem cell therapy is not approved by the FDA, but in Colombia it is legal. Days 4 and 5: exosomes IV and nebulized. The neurologist reviews your case first and decides whether this route is for you.
